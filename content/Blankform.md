A journal of my experiences learning design and starting my own personal design studio [Blankform](http://blankform.co).

#### November 4th, 2025
For the past two years, the changing of fall to winter has been a time of significant reflection for me.  Around this time, two years ago, I returned from my solo-backpacking trip in China—22 and starry-eyed.  I had just graduated college as a Computer Science and Business-Economics major.  No job lined up, no prospects yet inexplicably hopeful for the future.

Almost immediately after the new year, I landed my first internship at a startup.  3 months later, I quit.

It has almost been a year and a half since then and I’ve felt every second of it.  

You know what’s funny?  Whenever I’m reading biographies, a year never seems like a long time.  Oh, he spent a year learning calligraphy which helped him create the first digital font?  Cool!  

The year almost becomes an afterthought to the achievement.

But living through it? Damn can a year feel like a long time.  

Ever since I’ve left my job, I’ve watched all of my friends go into industry, make good money, and establish roots in places once foreign.  Some of them use the money to enjoy their twenties.  Some of them invest the money to set themselves up for life.  All of them have an income, their own place to live, and a steady path forward.

I, on the otherhand, have been spending the last 12 months at cafes, learning, studying, fighting the lack of structure that others can so easily rely on.  I’ve got none of what they have, and nothing to show for my efforts either.  No clients, no money, no income, no connections.

I’m writing this now because I want to remember how I felt during this period.  If I succeed, I want others who want to go down the same path to know that all of *this* is normal. That it really is just part of the process.

Everyone today seems to be focused on “results”.  Employers need their employees to create “value” in the shortest time possible.  Marketers know to sell the end goal rather than the journey to get there.  But nobody seems to talk about the work required to achieve those results.  Over the past year and a half of my journey into **design**, I’ve had *nothing* to show for it.  No recognition online, nobody liking my work, absolutely nada.  I’ve been left alone in a room with my own standards, constantly disappointing and failing to meet his expectations.

I’m writing this today because, after this year and a half of work with no applause, I’ve finally managed to land my first client.  Unpaid, but a real person that believes in my designs nonetheless.  After a year and a half of nothing, I finally have something.

#### December 10th, 2025 
Working with a client that has a target aesthetic that is *completely* outside of my comfort zone made me realize a couple things about good design.  They want a mix between editorial and eclectic imagery like ethnographic rugs, medieval illumination graphics, etc.

Now that’s a hard mix to capture.

I’ve learned 1) not to ask clients for too much input because although they might know what they like, they don’t know what belongs on a website.  They’re paying me to design, so why am I asking them to do the design direction?  2) There is an aesthetic for everything.  Getting it out of them may be hard, but they do have an aesthetic that speaks to them.  3) **Restraint is good design**.  Not in the minimalist/constructivist sense, but in the fact that if you try to embody an aesthetic with **everything** in the design, it will feel overwhelming—a feeling that most clients do not actively seek out.

For example, here, I’m trying to incorporate ethnographic imagery into the main typography, primary colors, *and* imagery.    It feels too jarring for a webpage.

Also using their desired color palettes result in terrible results.

##### January 22nd, 2026
> **Designing for others feels different than designing for yourself**

Putting your design into the world feels much different than designing in private.  So much of designing someone else’s dream brand is leading them through the process. In private, you understand your process, you know all of the effort you’re putting into each of the design decisions.  When you’re working with someone else, they know none of this.

The most important part of designing for others is leading them through the winding process without them feeling lost anywhere along the way.  And this is **much** harder said than done.

When you get a client, they’re almost always going to be focused on the end result.  It’s important to show them you’re making progress towards this end result every step of the way.

You must be very particular about how you communicate in order to succeed.

I sat in the tool for an hour, hovering above buttons and testing stuff.  At first, it felt fucking terrible.  To the people around me, it probably looked like I was designing for the first time.  Or perhaps touching a keyboard for the first time.
