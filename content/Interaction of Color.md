#### Visual Memory/Color Recollection 
> If one says "Red" ( the name of a color) and there are 50 people listening, it can be expected that there will be 50 reds in their minds.  And one can be sure that all these reds will be very different.

Visual memory is very poor.  If you asked someone to pick out **Coca Cola Red**, you’d get many different answers.  Even if presented with the logo mark itself, a large majority of people would still get the shade of red wrong.

This is because **color is the most relative** element of design.  It depends widely on its context and surroundings and appears differently to every single person.

> First, it is hard, if not impossible, to remember distinct colors. This underscores the important fact that the visual memory is very poor in comparison with our auditory memory. Often the latter is able to repeat a melody heard only once or twice.

> Second, the nomenclature of color is most inadequate. Though there are innumerable colors-shades and tones- in daily vocabulary, there are only about 30 color names.

#### Color reading and contexture 
In the 80s with the rise of [[Constructivism]], there used to be a common misconception that “the simpler the form of the letter, the simpler its reading” which is the principle most leading typographers at the time believed in.

Today, with studies done in the field of [[Gestalt Psychology]], we know that this is not true.   When reading text, ==we do not read each letter, but instead in [[word pictures]]==. 

> Ophthalmology has disclosed that the more the letters are differentiated from each other, the easier is the reading.

This results in a particular effect when reading fully capitalized words—the even heights and lack of distinction prevents our eyes from glancing the word picture, forcing our eyes to move along the length of the word.

#### A Color has Many Different Faces 
When placing a **warm** bucket of water, a **lukewarm** bucket of water, and a **cold** bucket of water in a row right next to each other, a person can experience a **haptic illusion** by moving down the row in different directions.

If a person dips their hand into the **warm** bucket of water, they will feel a **warm sensation**.  When another person dips their hand into the **cold** bucket of water, they will feel a **cold sensation**.  However, when both people dip their hands into the **lukewarm** bucket of water, they will experience **different sensations**—*cold* and *warm* except in different directions.  

However, neither of **warm** or **cold** are the physical temperature of the water, which is namely **lukewarm**.  This discrepancy between **physical fact** and **psychic effect** is known as a **haptic illusion**.

Similarly to haptics, we experience these illusions when looking at subjects as well—==optical illusions==.

#### Lighter or Darker — Light Intensity, Lightness
One who cannot tell the difference between a high tone and a low tone should probably stay away from producing music.

However, the same cannot be said about colors.  Very few people can actually tell between higher and lower intensity between different hues (usually called higher and lower value).
