[[The Brand Gap]] by Marty Neumeier is the book that introduced me to brand strategy and how to think about branding from a strategic point of view.

##### Differentiation
> Different, I Like It

I’ve realized that a large portion of what makes good design is **adding differences**.  A webpage with uniform spacing, font, text-size isn’t very interesting or cognitively stimulating.  As humans, our brain has evolved to **pattern match** on autopilot.  We are constantly grouping stimuli together in order to lessen the noise.

This is also why **we tend to like things that are different**.  Something different stands out from the crowd and grabs our attention.

> The Evolution of Design

Design used to have four main goals in mind: to identify, to inform, to entertain, and to persuade.   Neumeier says branding adds a fifth: **to differentiate**.  While the first four are tactical, the fifth is strategic ([[Strategic vs Tactical|note on the difference between strategic and tactical]]).  Neumeier says that the fifth is ==deeply rooted in aesthetics==, containing a mix of logic and magic.

> Design and Tribalism

In the past, there arose an idea of a “global village” that made all world divisions obsolete.  Almost half a century later, the division between countries are very much still existent.  Instead, technology has become a blanket that connects the entire world and adds nuance to these boundaries.

Today, more than ever, people are looking for a place to belong.   We’re seeing the formation of **tribes** all across the world.  And it’s almost always been this way—without a tribe, you are left alone, mostly defenseless against whatever dangers might appear.

Marketing has evolved to reflect this change — marketing has transitioned from “what it has” to “what it does” to “what it feels like” finally to “who you are”.

Brands play a large role in reating these tribes.  You can join any number of tribes with brands on any day at any time.  Some popular examples today are:
>	“Oh you do Duolingo too?”
>	“Oh you climb at Movement too?”
>	“Oh you also go to lifetime?”

Brands play a large role in creating shared spaces for people to interact.  Yet the consumers still are in control.  If a brand falls from grace, they can swap to the next one.

==A Note on Marketing==
Marketing used to be centered around your **Unique Selling Proposition** (USP).  However, today a Unique Selling Proposition might not be enough.   Unique Selling Propositions focus on features and what your product has to offer.  Today’s world of selling to a **person** might require a different approach, one centered on the customer’s **world view and current state of being**.  Thus, Marty references a **Unique Buying State**.

> Focus, Focus, Focus

These are the three most important words in branding.  Most brands do not suffer from too much focus, but rather from a lack of it.

HIstorical data shows that it pays off to be number one and two in a category.  Number three, four, and five may only be worth pursuing if there’s a chance at becoming number one or two someday.  The market leader can charge a premium while, for others, differentiation might need to include lower prices.

The brand landscape runs by “survival of the fittingest”.  Usually, becoming number one for a smaller audience is more handsomely rewarded than third for a larger market. Successful brands won’t wait for categories to arise, they create their own.  They know what they are, what makes it different, and why people want it.

==Competition forces specialization==

> Are you harvesting or growing your brand?

Much of branding is about planting the seeds for the future while harvesting what you’ve grown now.  Premium brands can create product line extensions and usually do very well with them in the short term.  But, unless done strategically, these extensions can uproot the existing brand in exchange for short term profits.

An example is Porsche creating a SUV.  They were known for creating classic sports cars and extending their line to a SUV blurs the image they’ve established.

Even in the best of times, the principle of focus is a hard mistress, demanding fidelity, courage, and determination.

In the world of brands, it’s **differentiate or die.**

##### Collaboration 
> The different types of Brand Villages

1. One stop shop
2. Brand Agency
3. Integrated Branding

The one stop shop was where

The brand agency is where a single firm assembles specialists to create and develop a brand.  Usually the benefit of working with a brand agency is the benefit of best-in-class specialists.  However, the downside is that the brand knowledge walks out the door with the last agency that worked on it.

Modern day companies are moving towards an “unbundled economy” where, instead of trying to own an entire vertical in-house, they hire specialists to create value faster, in-parallel.  The example Marty points to is Hollywood, where we’ve seen a marked transition from large studios to specialized studios that are hired onto projects.

> The Power of Prototypes

**Branding is about the *gut feel* a consumer gets when they see your product.**  This makes prototypes invaluable as it allows the right side of the brain to do the work while also allowing everyone to align on a direction.  By being “close to life”, prototypes allow clients to understand whether a brand will fall or live in the real-world without the pressure of a real release.

Similar to prototype screenplays in hollywood, this allows stakeholders to make any major edits before  money is invested into the product.  Once a script is agreed upon, it also keeps everyone on the same page as the project moves forward.
