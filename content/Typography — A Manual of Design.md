A book that covers the art of **setting type**—wielding the fonts created by type foundries.  Ruder distills the lessons from 25 years of teaching typesetting into this work.

##### Point, Line, and Surface
> *Everything is movement: the dot moves and gives rise to the
> line, the line moves and produces a plane surface, and plane
> surfaces come together and create a body.*

The effect will disappear when you isolate only the line, but reappear when you look at the scene as a whole.

##### Contrasts
> ==The relationship between the printed and the unprinted area must be one of tension, and this tension comes about through contrasts. Values combined with equal values result in unrelieved monotony.==

Much of design is ensuring that there is a natural rhythm to things.  Everything in nature has a natural rhythm.   A telltale sign of a young designer is the lack of rhythm, the abundance of equality and monotony.

> *Thinking in terms of contrasts is not a confused way of thinking, for even contrasts can be united in a harmonious whole. There are concepts which become real only through their opposite, e. g. "'above", in conjunction with "below", "horizontal" in conjunction with "vertical", etc. Modern man thinks in contrasts. For him surface and space, far and near, inner and outer are no longer incompatible; for him there is not only an "either-or" but also a "both-and"*
##### Shades of Gray
Typographers usually describe type in terms of **shades of gray**, the “typographic gray”, as they call it.   This gray is composed of all features of a font, including tracking and leading. 

![[typographic-gray-visualization.png]]

While looking directly at set type, it may be hard to distinguish the **gray** that Ruder and other typographers refer to.  However, if you zoom it, it becomes easier to see.  In the picture above, there are clearly 3 different values of gray, resulting from varying values of line-height.
